Historical Urban Event Evidence Data Catalog for
New York City and London
Executive summary
This catalog identifies the strongest official and quasi-official data sources available through April 2026 for
building an open, before/after urban event evidence platform focused on New York City and London. The
two cities are both unusually well covered, but their strengths differ. New York City is stronger on open lot-
level land use, permits, certificates of occupancy, 311 complaints, parcel geometry, and a long official
orthophoto record. London is stronger on centralized planning approvals and progress monitoring through
the Planning London Datahub, borough and national statistical series, and national property market data.
Both cities benefit from national or global layers for satellite imagery, census and employment, and
OpenStreetMap history.
The highest-value MVP stack is clear. For New York City, the strongest core is NYC Open Data,
Metropolitan Transportation Authority, and U.S. Census Bureau, especially orthophotos,
MapPLUTO/PLUTO, Digital Tax Map layers, DOB filings and permits, street geometry, traffic counts, 311,
LEHD/LODES, ACS, and Department of Finance sales and assessment data. For London, the core is Greater
London Authority, Transport for London, Office for National Statistics, Ordnance Survey, HM
Land Registry, and Historic England, especially the Planning London Datahub, London Development
Database archive, TfL APIs and demand datasets, DfT traffic counts, ONS/Nomis, Land Registry property
series, and Historic England’s aerial photo archive.
A practical caveat matters: “2000–2026 coverage” does not mean each series has published complete 2026
data. In most official annual statistical systems, 2026 is only partial or not yet released as of April 2026. Live
APIs and continuously updated portals do have 2026 records, but annual surveys and administrative rollups
often stop in 2024 or 2025. A production platform should therefore model event time separately from
publication time and preserve vintage metadata for every record family.

Foundational datasets that matter in both cities
The most portable cross-city layer set comes from U.S. Geological Survey, the Copernicus program, and
OpenStreetMap. These sources are not city-specific, but they make the platform robust when city data is
missing, delayed, or inconsistent. Landsat provides a long public-domain archive useful for urban heat,
surface temperature, coarse land-cover change, water, and greenness. Sentinel-2 provides higher-resolution
multispectral imagery from 2015 onward, free and open to global users. OpenStreetMap adds change
history from the mid-2000s onward and is especially useful for roads, buildings, amenities, and user-
contributed edits, provided attribution and share-alike obligations are respected.

Implementation guidance, visualizations, and legal and ethical
constraints
A production-grade platform should model every source using a small set of adapter patterns. The most
useful are TILER for imagery and basemaps, SNAPSHOT for annual or periodic full extracts such as PLUTO
or PTAL, LEDGER for event streams such as permits, complaints, and ridership, DOCSTORE for plans,
consultation documents, comment files, and environmental review documents, and LINKER for stable
identifiers such as BBL, BIN, station IDs, USRN, and UPRN. These adapters should write into a unified
56
57
21

spatiotemporal graph keyed by event location, event time, publication time, and source vintage. In New
York City the natural location key is usually BBL/BIN plus street segment and station complex linkages. In
London the practical key is usually UPRN/USRN plus LPA application IDs, NaPTAN/StopPoint IDs, or DfT
count points.
The most effective visual products are not generic dashboards. They are tightly linked, event-centric views: a
synchronized before/after image swipe using official imagery; an event timeline showing filings,
approvals, permits, starts, completions, and occupancy; distance-decay charts of traffic, ridership,
employment, complaints, and prices around the event; isochrone/PTAL or transit-access panels; property
and rent trend panels; and a document evidence tab containing planning documents, public comments,
and environmental statements. NYC is especially suited to complaint and transit ridership deltas. London is
especially suited to planning lifecycle tracking, transit-access summaries, and official transaction/rent trend
overlays.
Interpolation strategies are necessary whenever a dataset does not span the whole 2000–2026 window. For
imagery, fill missing years with Landsat or Sentinel seasonal composites between official aerial captures.
For London building-level geometry change, infer change windows from PLD/LDD applications, OS/
OpenStreetMap baselines, and historic aerials when a fully open historical building footprint series is
missing. For 2026 statistics that are not yet published, use the latest completed annual vintage and pair it
with live operational APIs only when those live metrics are conceptually comparable. For labour-market or
rent series with publication lags, the safest method is to report the latest official year and show 2026 only as
provisional or “live operational context,” not as directly equivalent annual statistics.
The legal and ethical constraints are real. NYC Open Data broadly states that there are no general
restrictions on use, but that does not eliminate privacy or defamation risk when combining complaint text,
addresses, and sensitive topics. TfL data are released under transport-data terms based on the OGL but
with specific amendments, so commercial republication, branding, caching, and refresh behavior should be
checked source by source. London Datastore licenses vary at the package level. OS OpenData products are
OGL and relatively easy to reuse, but premium OS imagery and some 3D products are not equivalent to
open city data. Historic England imagery can have image-specific rights and attributions. OpenStreetMap
requires attribution and ODbL share-alike treatment for derivative databases. Planning comments can
contain personal data or allegations; an evidence platform should minimize personal-text display and
privilege aggregation, excerpting, and document links over wholesale republication.
Open questions and limitations
This report is comprehensive on the highest-confidence official layers, but a few areas remain structurally
weaker and should be treated as partial rather than complete. London does not have a clean equivalent to
NYC’s lot-level open parcel-and-building stack or a universal 311-type city service-request ledger. NYC has
strong public administrative and complaint data, but official open rent series are more fragmented than
London’s Land Registry plus VOA stack. I also did not verify every historical PLUTO/MapPLUTO vintage
individually or every pre-2010 London borough planning portal archive in this pass; those are better
handled during implementation by a one-time availability crawl.
For a public open-source release, the safest position is to start with the high-confidence official core in
this report, mark 2026 annual series as partial, keep a reproducible vintage registry, and add secondary
